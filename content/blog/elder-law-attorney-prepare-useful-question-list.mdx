# Elder Law Attorney: How to Prepare a Useful Question List

Walking into a first conversation with an elder law attorney can feel like standing at the edge of a very large room with no map. There is a lot you may want to ask, and the clock is usually ticking. A prepared question list is one of the simplest tools you can bring. It keeps the conversation focused on what matters to you, helps you compare providers on equal terms, and reduces the chance that you leave wishing you had asked something important.

This guide is about how to build that list. It is not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for a consultation with a qualified professional. Think of it as a planning framework you can adapt to your own situation.

## Why a question list matters for provider selection

Elder law touches several areas at once: long-term care planning, powers of attorney, advance directives, guardianship questions, benefits coordination, and family decision-making. A single meeting can wander across all of them. Without a list, the conversation tends to follow whatever is most familiar to the person answering questions rather than what is most urgent for you.

A written list does three things:

1. **It sets your agenda.** You decide which topics get airtime first.
2. **It makes comparison possible.** If you speak with more than one provider, the same questions give you a fair basis to compare how each one responds.
3. **It surfaces what you do not yet know.** Gaps in your own understanding often appear when you try to write the question down.

## Start with your situation, not with the law

Before drafting questions about legal tools, spend ten minutes writing a plain-language summary of your circumstances. This summary becomes the backbone of your list.

Consider these prompts:

- Who is the planning for? You, a parent, a spouse, or a broader family group?
- What is prompting the search right now? A recent diagnosis, a move, a change in care needs, or simply getting organized?
- What decisions feel most pressing in the next three months?
- What decisions can wait longer?
- Who else needs to be part of the conversation, and are they available?

**Example (hypothetical, for illustration only):** A family is helping a parent who recently moved into an assisted living community. The pressing question is how to organize decision-making authority if the parent's health changes. A less urgent question is how to think about long-term asset planning. Both belong on the list, but in different positions.

## A checklist for building your question list

Use this checklist as a drafting tool. You do not need every item, and the order can change.

### 1. Scope and experience

- What areas of elder law does the provider focus on most often?
- How would you describe your typical approach to a situation like mine?
- Are there parts of my situation that fall outside your practice, and how would you handle that?

### 2. Process and communication

- What does the first few steps of working together look like?
- How will you keep me informed, and how often should I expect updates?
- Who on your team will I actually be speaking with?
- How do you prefer to receive questions between meetings?

### 3. Documents and preparation

- What should I bring to a first meeting?
- What information would help you give me a useful answer?
- Is there anything I should gather from other family members first?

### 4. Decision-making and roles

- If multiple family members are involved, how do you usually work with them?
- How do you handle disagreements within a family?
- What role do you expect me to play in gathering information?

### 5. Planning considerations

- What kinds of planning tools do you commonly discuss, and why?
- How do you decide which options are worth exploring for a given situation?
- What should I understand about how these tools interact with each other?

### 6. Follow-up and next steps

- What would a written summary of our conversation include?
- What are the next two or three steps you would recommend?
- When should we speak again?

## Turning the checklist into a real list

A checklist is a menu. A question list is your order. Trim the checklist down to the questions that matter most to you, then rephrase them in your own words. Questions in your own words are easier to ask and easier to remember.

Aim for eight to twelve questions. More than that and you may not get through them. If you have more, group them under headings and mark the top three as must-ask.

**Example (hypothetical, for illustration only):** A person preparing for a conversation might write: "I want to understand how decision-making documents work together if my mother's health changes suddenly. I also want to know what information you need from me before we can talk about that." That is two questions, both specific, both answerable.

## Questions that tend to be less useful

Some questions sound reasonable but rarely produce helpful answers in a first conversation:

- "What is the best option for me?" This invites a conclusion before the provider has enough context.
- "How much will this cost?" without describing the situation. Cost depends on scope, and scope depends on facts.
- "Can you guarantee a particular outcome?" No responsible professional can promise outcomes.
- "What do most people do?" Popularity is not the same as fit.

Replace these with questions about process, scope, and how decisions get made. Those tend to produce information you can actually use.

## Preparing to compare more than one provider

If you plan to speak with more than one provider, keep your core questions identical across conversations. That way, differences in the answers reflect differences in approach rather than differences in what you asked.

After each conversation, write down:

- Which questions were answered directly
- Which questions were deferred, and why
- How clearly the provider explained their process
- Whether you felt your situation was understood

These notes are for your own comparison. They are not a rating of any provider, and they are not a substitute for your own judgment.

## A short pre-conversation routine

Before the meeting, try this:

1. Re-read your situation summary.
2. Read your question list out loud once.
3. Mark your top three questions.
4. Set aside a page for notes.
5. Decide in advance what a useful outcome from the conversation would look like.

That last step matters. A useful outcome might be clarity about next steps, a better understanding of your options, or simply knowing what information to gather. It does not have to be a final decision.

## After the conversation

Give yourself time to process. Review your notes the same day if you can, while the conversation is fresh. Add any new questions that came up. If something was unclear, write it down and decide whether to follow up.

If you are working with more than one provider, resist the urge to decide immediately. Let the notes settle for a day or two. The question list you built is not just a tool for the meeting. It is a record of what you cared about going in, and that record is useful when you look back.
