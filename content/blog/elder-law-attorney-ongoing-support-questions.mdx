## Why ongoing support deserves its own conversation

Many people choose an elder law attorney during a moment of urgency. A health crisis, a move into a care setting, or a sudden change in a family member's needs can push a family to seek help quickly. In that rush, it is easy to focus only on the immediate task: getting a will signed, setting up a power of attorney, or reviewing a care contract.

But elder law is rarely a one-time event. Needs change. Documents that made sense last year may need revision. A parent's health may shift. A caregiver may burn out. A family may need to revisit how decisions are made, who is authorized to act, and what happens next.

That is why it helps to ask about ongoing support before you commit to a provider. This guide offers a practical set of questions, a checklist you can bring to a consultation, and hypothetical examples that are clearly labeled as examples. It is not legal advice, and it does not replace a consultation with a qualified professional in your jurisdiction.

## What "ongoing support" can mean in elder law

Ongoing support is not a single service. It can include:

- Periodic reviews of documents and plans as circumstances change
- Help updating powers of attorney, advance directives, or trusts
- Guidance when a family member's capacity or care needs change
- Coordination with other professionals, such as financial advisors or care managers
- Communication with family members who share caregiving responsibilities
- Availability for questions that come up between major events
- A clear process for reaching the attorney or the attorney's team

Because these services vary by firm and by jurisdiction, the goal is not to assume a standard package. The goal is to understand what a specific provider offers, how it is delivered, and how you would access it.

## Core questions to ask about ongoing support

### 1. How do you handle follow-up after the initial plan is complete?

Ask what happens after the first set of documents is signed. Do you schedule a review? Is there a standard timeline, or is it based on the client's situation? If nothing changes, will we hear from you, or should we reach out?

A clear answer helps you understand whether the relationship is designed to be episodic or continuing.

### 2. What triggers a review or update?

Circumstances that often prompt a review include a change in health, a move, a change in marital status, a death in the family, or a significant change in finances. Ask which of these the firm typically monitors or asks about, and how you should notify them.

### 3. How do we contact you between major events?

Ask about the practical channels: phone, email, a client portal, or scheduled check-ins. Also ask about response times. You are not looking for a guarantee, but for a realistic sense of how communication works.

### 4. Who on your team will we work with?

Some firms have paralegals, care coordinators, or other staff who handle routine questions. Ask who does what, and whether you will primarily speak with the attorney or with a team member.

### 5. How do you coordinate with other professionals?

Elder law often overlaps with financial planning, health care, and social services. Ask whether the attorney is willing to coordinate with other professionals you already work with, and how that coordination typically happens.

### 6. What should we do if something changes suddenly?

Emergencies happen. Ask what the process is if a hospitalization, a crisis, or an urgent decision occurs. You are not asking for a promise of immediate availability, but for a clear process.

### 7. How do you keep records and share updates?

Ask how documents are stored, how updates are communicated, and whether you will receive copies of anything that changes. This matters for family members who share responsibility.

### 8. What are the limits of your ongoing support?

Every provider has boundaries. Ask what is included in a typical engagement and what would be a separate matter. This helps you avoid surprises and helps you plan.

### 9. How do you handle changes in the client's ability to participate?

If a client's capacity changes, the attorney's role may shift. Ask how the firm approaches these situations and what family members should know in advance.

### 10. What questions should we be asking that we have not asked yet?

This open-ended question can reveal assumptions you did not know you had. It also gives the attorney a chance to explain what they see as the most important considerations for your situation.

## A practical checklist you can bring

Use this checklist as a starting point. Add your own questions based on your family's circumstances.

- [ ] Ask how follow-up is scheduled and whether it is automatic or client-initiated.
- [ ] Ask what life changes should trigger a call to the attorney.
- [ ] Ask about the preferred method of contact and typical response times.
- [ ] Ask who will handle routine questions and who will handle legal decisions.
- [ ] Ask whether the attorney coordinates with other professionals.
- [ ] Ask what to do in an urgent situation.
- [ ] Ask how documents and updates are stored and shared.
- [ ] Ask what is included in ongoing support and what is separate.
- [ ] Ask how the firm handles changes in a client's capacity or participation.
- [ ] Ask what else you should be thinking about.

## Hypothetical examples (for illustration only)

The following examples are hypothetical. They are not real client stories, and they do not describe any specific provider. They are meant to show how the questions above might play out in conversation.

**Example 1: The annual review question.**

A family asks, "Do you schedule an annual review, or should we call you?" The attorney explains that the firm sends a reminder each year, but also encourages clients to call if something changes. The family notes this in their checklist and asks what would count as a change worth calling about. The attorney lists a few examples, such as a move or a new diagnosis. The family leaves with a clearer sense of when to reach out.

**Example 2: The communication channel question.**

A caregiver asks, "If I have a quick question, should I email you or call the office?" The attorney explains that routine questions go to a paralegal, while legal questions come to the attorney. The caregiver asks how long responses usually take. The attorney gives a general range and notes that urgent matters should be marked clearly. The caregiver now knows what to expect.

**Example 3: The coordination question.**

A family already works with a financial advisor. They ask whether the attorney is willing to coordinate with that advisor. The attorney says yes, with the client's permission, and describes how that usually works. The family asks what information would be shared and how. This helps them decide whether the fit is right.

**Example 4: The capacity question.**

An adult child asks, "What happens if my parent can no longer make decisions?" The attorney explains that the answer depends on the documents in place and the specific circumstances, and that this is exactly why ongoing reviews matter. The attorney suggests reviewing the current documents together. The family adds this to their list of next steps.

These examples are simplified. Real situations involve more detail and depend on local law and individual facts. They are included only to show how the questions might sound in practice.

## How to use what you learn

After a consultation, take a few minutes to compare what you heard with what matters most to your family. Consider:

- Did the provider give clear answers, or did they avoid specifics?
- Did they explain their process in a way you could repeat to another family member?
- Did they acknowledge the limits of their role and encourage you to consult other professionals where appropriate?
- Did they make space for your questions, including the ones you had not thought to ask?

There is no single right answer. Different families need different levels of support. The goal is to find a provider whose approach matches your situation and your preferences.

## When to consult a qualified professional

This article is a general guide. It is not legal advice, and it does not create an attorney-client relationship. Laws vary by state and by situation. If you are making decisions about an elder law matter, consult a qualified elder law attorney in your jurisdiction. If you have questions about health care or caregiving, consult appropriate health care or social service professionals.
