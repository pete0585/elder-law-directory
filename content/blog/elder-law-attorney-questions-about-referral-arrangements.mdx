# Elder Law Attorney: Questions About Referral Arrangements

When you begin searching for an elder law attorney, one of the first names you encounter may come from a referral. A friend, a financial advisor, a hospital discharge planner, a senior living community, or an online directory may point you toward a specific lawyer. Referrals can be genuinely helpful. They can also raise questions that are worth asking before you schedule a consultation or sign an engagement letter.

This guide focuses on those questions. It is written for people researching elder law attorney services and for family members helping an older adult find legal support. It does not offer legal advice and does not evaluate any named provider. Instead, it offers a practical decision framework and a checklist you can adapt to your own situation. Where a question touches on licensing rules, fee-sharing rules, or professional conduct standards, the appropriate step is to consult a qualified professional in your jurisdiction.

## Why Referral Arrangements Matter

Elder law often involves interconnected decisions: long-term care planning, powers of attorney, advance directives, guardianship or conservatorship questions, benefits counseling, and estate matters. Because the issues cross several domains, professionals in adjacent fields frequently refer clients to one another. An elder law attorney may receive referrals from financial planners, geriatric care managers, social workers, accountants, or senior housing advisors. The attorney may also refer clients outward to those same professionals.

A referral arrangement can be informal — a professional simply knows someone they trust — or more structured. Structured arrangements can include preferred-provider lists, co-marketing, or formal business relationships. The key question for a consumer is not whether a referral exists, but whether the referral serves your interests and whether any relationship between the referrer and the attorney is disclosed clearly enough for you to make an informed choice.

## Questions to Ask About an Incoming Referral

When someone recommends an elder law attorney to you, consider asking the referrer:

- **How do you know this attorney?** A personal or professional relationship is not disqualifying, but knowing its nature helps you weigh the recommendation.
- **Have you referred other clients or family members to this attorney?** This can reveal whether the recommendation is based on repeated experience.
- **Do you receive any compensation, credit, or benefit from this referral?** If the answer is yes, ask for details. If the answer is unclear, treat that as a signal to do your own independent research.
- **What specifically did this attorney help you or your client with?** A general reputation is less useful than a concrete description of the work.
- **Would you refer this attorney to your own family member?** This question often surfaces honest hesitations.

If the referral comes from a hospital, skilled nursing facility, or senior living community, you may also ask whether the facility has a formal relationship with the attorney or a preferred-provider list. Facilities sometimes maintain lists of professionals who have agreed to certain service standards. That is not inherently a problem, but you should understand the basis of the list.

## Questions to Ask the Attorney Directly

Once you contact the attorney, you can ask about referral arrangements directly. Many attorneys expect these questions and answer them as part of an initial conversation.

- **How did you come to be on this referral list or receive this referral?**
- **Do you have any business relationship with the person or organization that referred me?**
- **Do you pay for referrals, or do you receive payment for referring clients to other professionals?** Rules about fee-sharing and referral fees vary by jurisdiction and by profession, so ask the attorney to explain the applicable standards where they practice.
- **Do you refer clients to other professionals? If so, how do you select them?**
- **If I need a service you do not provide, how will you help me find it?**
- **Will you tell me in writing about any relationship that could affect your recommendation?**

You are not expected to interrogate anyone. A brief, direct conversation is usually enough. Attorneys who are transparent about referral relationships tend to welcome the questions.

## A Practical Checklist

Use this checklist as you evaluate a referral and the attorney on the other end of it.

**About the referral source**
- [ ] I know how the referrer knows the attorney.
- [ ] I have asked whether the referrer receives any benefit from the referral.
- [ ] I understand whether the referral is part of a formal list or arrangement.
- [ ] I have considered whether the referrer's interests and mine are aligned.

**About the attorney**
- [ ] I have asked how the attorney receives referrals.
- [ ] I have asked about any business relationship with the referrer.
- [ ] I have asked how the attorney handles referrals to other professionals.
- [ ] I have confirmed that the attorney will disclose conflicts or relationships that could affect my case.
- [ ] I have asked what happens if I want a second opinion or a different attorney.

**About my own decision**
- [ ] I am comparing more than one option when possible.
- [ ] I understand the scope of services being offered.
- [ ] I know what questions to ask about costs and availability.
- [ ] I feel comfortable asking follow-up questions.
- [ ] I know that I can consult a qualified professional if I need advice specific to my situation.

## Hypothetical Examples

*The examples below are hypothetical and clearly labeled as examples. They are not descriptions of real people, providers, or outcomes.*

**Example 1: The financial advisor's recommendation.**
Suppose a financial advisor suggests an elder law attorney to a client who is planning for long-term care. The advisor mentions that the attorney often works with clients on benefits planning. In this hypothetical, the client might ask the advisor whether they receive any compensation for the referral, and then ask the attorney the same question. If both answers are consistent and no compensation is involved, the client can weigh the recommendation on its merits. If the answers differ, that is useful information.

**Example 2: The facility's preferred-provider list.**
Imagine a skilled nursing facility gives a family a printed list of elder law attorneys. The list includes a note that these attorneys have agreed to certain response times. In this hypothetical, the family could ask the facility how attorneys are added to or removed from the list, and then ask an attorney on the list about their relationship with the facility. The family might also choose to speak with an attorney not on the list to compare.

**Example 3: The attorney's own referral outward.**
Suppose an elder law attorney tells a client that they need a geriatric care manager. The attorney provides two names. In this hypothetical, the client could ask how the attorney selected those names, whether the attorney has any financial relationship with them, and whether the client is free to choose someone else. A clear answer helps the client decide how much weight to give the recommendation.

**Example 4: The online directory.**
Imagine someone finds an attorney through an online directory. The directory may include paid listings, editorial selections, or both. In this hypothetical, the reader could check whether the directory explains how listings are selected and whether attorneys pay to appear. The reader could then verify the attorney's background through independent sources and a direct conversation.

These examples are meant to illustrate the kinds of questions that can be useful. They are not predictions about what you will encounter, and they are not endorsements of any approach.

## What Referral Arrangements Do Not Tell You

A referral, even a strong one, does not tell you everything. It does not tell you whether the attorney has the specific experience your situation requires. It does not tell you how the attorney communicates, how they handle deadlines, or how they work with families in conflict. It does not tell you whether the attorney's approach matches your values or your budget.

## When to Seek Professional Guidance

Referral arrangements can involve professional conduct rules, fee-sharing rules, and disclosure obligations that differ by jurisdiction and by profession. This article cannot tell you what is required where you live or what a specific arrangement means for your case. If you have concerns about whether a referral arrangement is appropriate, or if you need advice about your own legal situation, consult a qualified professional. An elder law attorney, a state bar association's ethics hotline, or another licensed professional in your jurisdiction may be able to help.

## A Final Note on Taking Your Time

A referral is a starting point, not a final answer. You are allowed to ask questions, compare options, and take the time you need. The goal is not to find the attorney with the most impressive referral network. The goal is to find an attorney whose experience, communication style, and approach fit your situation — and to understand the relationships that brought you to them.

If a referral arrangement is disclosed clearly and the attorney answers your questions directly, that is a useful signal. If the arrangement is unclear or the answers are evasive, that is also useful information. Either way, you are the one making the decision.
