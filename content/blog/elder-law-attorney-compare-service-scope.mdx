## Start With Scope, Not Slogans

When people begin researching elder law attorneys, the first thing they often see is a list of practice areas: estate planning, Medicaid planning, guardianship, probate, long-term care. Those words are a useful starting point, but they can also blur together. Two providers may both list "Medicaid planning" while offering very different levels of involvement, documentation, and follow-through.

Comparing service scope means asking a more precise question: what does this provider actually do, for whom, and how far does their involvement extend? This guide offers a structured way to compare providers on scope alone, without relying on rankings, prices, or promises. It is general information, not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for a consultation with a qualified professional in your jurisdiction.

## Why Scope Matters More Than a Single Label

Elder law touches many areas of life: housing, health care decision-making, financial management, family relationships, and public benefits. A provider may concentrate on one part of that picture or work across several. Neither approach is automatically better; the right fit depends on your situation.

Scope matters because it shapes:

- **What questions get asked.** A narrow engagement may focus on one document or one application. A broader engagement may include a review of related risks.
- **Who is involved.** Some matters involve only the older adult. Others involve family members, caregivers, or fiduciaries.
- **What happens after the first meeting.** Some engagements end with a delivered document or filing. Others include periodic check-ins or updates when circumstances change.
- **How transitions are handled.** If your needs change, a provider with a wider scope may be able to continue, while a narrower one may refer you elsewhere.

Because scope varies, a side-by-side comparison of service descriptions is more useful than comparing marketing language.

## A Service-Scope Checklist

Use the following checklist when reviewing a provider's public materials or preparing questions for a consultation. You can adapt it to your own situation.

### 1. Practice focus

- Does the provider describe a focus on older adults and their families?
- Which specific areas do they list (for example, planning documents, benefits applications, guardianship, probate, long-term care planning)?
- Do they describe situations they do not handle?

### 2. Engagement format

- Is the initial meeting described as a consultation, a planning session, or something else?
- Is there any indication of how long an engagement typically lasts?
- Do they describe whether work is one-time, project-based, or ongoing?

### 3. Information gathering

- What information do they ask you to bring or prepare?
- Do they describe reviewing existing documents or prior arrangements?
- Do they mention involving family members or other professionals?

### 4. Deliverables

- What does the provider say you will receive (for example, a written plan, drafted documents, a filed application, a summary letter)?
- Do they describe how you will be kept informed of progress?
- Do they explain what happens if a matter cannot be completed as expected?

### 5. Coordination

- Do they describe working with financial advisors, care managers, or health care providers?
- Do they mention referrals to other attorneys or specialists?
- Do they explain how they handle matters outside their scope?

### 6. Follow-up and changes

- Do they describe what happens if your circumstances change?
- Is there any indication of periodic review or check-ins?
- Do they explain how to reach them with follow-up questions?

### 7. Communication

- What communication methods do they describe (phone, email, portal, in-person)?
- Do they mention response-time expectations or office hours?
- Do they describe how they share updates?

This checklist is not exhaustive, and not every provider will address every item publicly. Its purpose is to help you notice where descriptions are specific and where they are vague.

## Comparing Two Providers: A Hypothetical Example

The following example is clearly hypothetical and is meant only to illustrate how the checklist might be used. It does not describe any real provider, person, or situation.

Imagine two providers, described only by their public materials.

**Provider A** lists elder law as one of several practice areas. Their materials describe drafting wills, powers of attorney, and advance directives. They mention that more complex matters may be referred to another firm. They do not describe ongoing review or coordination with other professionals.

**Provider B** describes a focus on older adults and their families. Their materials list planning documents, benefits-related questions, and long-term care planning. They describe an initial planning session, a written summary of options, and periodic check-ins. They mention coordinating with financial and care professionals when appropriate.

Using the checklist:

- On **practice focus**, Provider A appears broader but less concentrated; Provider B appears more concentrated on older adults.
- On **engagement format**, Provider B describes a structured process with follow-up; Provider A's description is less specific.
- On **deliverables**, Provider B names a written summary; Provider A names documents.
- On **coordination**, Provider B describes working with other professionals; Provider A mentions referrals out.

Neither description is inherently better. If your situation is simple and document-focused, Provider A's scope may be sufficient. If your situation involves multiple moving parts and you expect changes over time, Provider B's described scope may align more closely. The point is that the comparison is based on described scope, not on reputation or marketing.

## A Second Hypothetical: When Scope Does Not Match

Again, this is a hypothetical illustration, not a real case.

Suppose a family is helping an older adult who is preparing to move into a care setting. They want help understanding what documents and arrangements might be relevant. They contact a provider whose public materials focus on probate and estate administration. During the initial conversation, the provider explains that their scope centers on administering estates after a loss, and that the family's current questions fall outside that scope. The provider offers a referral to a colleague whose work focuses on planning and care transitions.

This outcome is not a failure. It is an example of scope matching working as intended: the provider was clear about what they do, and the family learned where to look next. When comparing providers, it can be helpful to ask directly, "Is my situation within your typical scope, and if not, who might be a better fit?"

## Questions to Bring to a Consultation

Once you have compared public descriptions, a consultation is an opportunity to clarify scope in your own words. Consider asking:

- "Based on what I've described, is this within your typical work?"
- "What would you expect to deliver, and in what form?"
- "How would you keep me informed as things progress?"
- "What would happen if my situation changes partway through?"
- "Do you coordinate with other professionals, and if so, how?"
- "What do you not handle that I might need elsewhere?"

These questions are general and are not legal advice. The answers will depend on your jurisdiction, your circumstances, and the provider's own practices.

## Common Pitfalls When Comparing Scope

- **Assuming a longer list means broader service.** A long list of practice areas does not necessarily mean deep involvement in each.
- **Assuming a narrow list means limited help.** A focused provider may offer more depth within their area.
- **Confusing marketing language with described process.** Look for specifics about what happens, not just what is offered.
- **Overlooking follow-up.** Scope includes what happens after the first deliverable.
- **Skipping the referral question.** Knowing who a provider refers to can be as useful as knowing what they do.

## Keeping Your Comparison Grounded

As you compare providers, keep your own priorities visible. Write down what matters most to you: clarity, responsiveness, coordination, or a specific area of focus. Then compare descriptions against those priorities rather than against each other in the abstract.

It can also help to revisit your notes after each conversation. Descriptions that seemed similar at first may look different once you have asked specific questions about process and follow-up.

## Summary

Comparing elder law attorney service scope is less about finding the longest list of practice areas and more about understanding what a provider actually does, how they work, and what happens after the first meeting. Use the checklist above, ask specific questions, and treat hypothetical examples as illustrations rather than predictions. With a structured comparison, you can make a more informed decision about which provider's described scope fits your needs.
