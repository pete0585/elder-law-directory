# Elder Law Attorney: In-Person vs. Remote Appointments — Questions to Ask

Choosing an Elder Law Attorney is a provider-selection decision, and one of the first practical questions many families face is deceptively simple: should the first meeting happen in person or remotely? The answer is not universal. It depends on the older adult's comfort, the documents involved, the complexity of the matter, and how the attorney's office actually runs remote meetings.

This guide is not legal advice and does not recommend a specific format for your situation. Instead, it offers a decision framework, a question checklist, and clearly labeled hypothetical examples so you can ask better questions and make a more informed choice. Where your circumstances involve legal rights, benefits, or documents, consult a qualified Elder Law Attorney in your jurisdiction.

## Why the Format Question Matters

An Elder Law Attorney may help with matters such as advance planning documents, long-term care considerations, benefits questions, and coordination with family members. The format of your appointments can affect how easily documents are reviewed, how well an older adult participates, and how family members stay informed.

In-person appointments can make it easier to handle original documents, observe non-verbal cues, and include an older adult who is more comfortable in a familiar, structured setting. Remote appointments can reduce travel burden, make it easier to include out-of-state family members, and offer scheduling flexibility.

Neither format is automatically better. The right choice is the one that fits the client's needs and the attorney's practice. Your job during provider selection is to understand how each office handles both formats — and to ask directly.

## A Decision Framework: Five Factors to Weigh

Use these five factors to organize your thinking before you contact any office.

1. **The older adult's participation and comfort.** Does the person the matter concerns prefer face-to-face conversation, or are they comfortable with video calls? Do they have hearing, vision, or cognitive considerations that make one format easier?
2. **The documents involved.** Are there original documents that need to be reviewed, signed, or witnessed? Some steps may require specific procedures that an office can explain.
3. **The complexity of the matter.** More complex situations may benefit from longer, more structured sessions. Ask how the office handles complexity in each format.
4. **Family and support network involvement.** If multiple people need to participate, remote meetings can reduce travel barriers — but only if everyone can use the technology.
5. **Technology access and reliability.** Do you have a reliable connection, a private space, and a device with a camera and microphone? If not, ask about alternatives.

## Questions to Ask Before Choosing a Format

Bring these questions to your first contact with any office. The answers will help you compare providers on equal terms.

### About In-Person Appointments

- How do you typically structure a first in-person meeting?
- What should we bring, and what should we leave at home?
- Can family members attend, and is there a limit on how many?
- Is the office accessible for mobility aids, and is parking or transit nearby?
- How much time should we plan for, including waiting and check-in?
- Are there steps in the process that must happen in person, and why?

### About Remote Appointments

- What platform do you use, and do we need to install anything in advance?
- How do you verify identity and confirm who is in the room?
- How do you handle documents that need review or signature?
- What happens if the connection drops or someone cannot join?
- Can we do a short test call before the real appointment?
- How do you include family members who are in different locations?

### About Both Formats

- Who is our main point of contact, and how do we reach them?
- How do you confirm what was discussed and what happens next?
- What is your process for follow-up questions between appointments?
- How do you handle scheduling changes or emergencies?
- What should we prepare before the appointment, regardless of format?

## A Practical Checklist

Before you decide on a format, work through this checklist. It is designed to be used alongside the questions above.

- [ ] I have identified the person the matter concerns and confirmed their format preference.
- [ ] I have asked whether any steps require an in-person visit.
- [ ] I have confirmed the office's remote platform and tested my own setup.
- [ ] I have asked how documents are shared, reviewed, and stored.
- [ ] I have confirmed who may attend and how they can join.
- [ ] I have asked about accessibility, parking, and travel time for in-person visits.
- [ ] I have asked how the office communicates between appointments.
- [ ] I have written down my top three questions for the first meeting.
- [ ] I understand that this is provider selection, not legal advice, and I will consult a qualified professional for legal guidance.

## Clearly Labeled Hypothetical Examples

The following examples are hypothetical and are included only to illustrate how the decision framework might be applied. They are not real cases, not recommendations, and not predictions of any outcome.

**Example 1 — The out-of-state sibling.**
*Hypothetical:* An older adult lives in one state, and two adult children live in different states. The family wants everyone to hear the same information at the same time. A remote appointment might reduce travel barriers, but the family would need to ask how the office verifies identity and handles documents across locations. The family might also ask whether a later in-person visit is needed for any step.

**Example 2 — The original documents.**
*Hypothetical:* A family has a folder of original documents and is unsure which ones matter. They might ask the office whether an in-person appointment is useful for reviewing originals, and whether remote review is possible for an initial conversation. The key question is not which format is better in general, but which steps the office handles in each format.

**Example 3 — The comfort-first older adult.**
*Hypothetical:* An older adult is more engaged in familiar surroundings and finds travel tiring. The family might ask whether a remote first meeting is possible, and whether a follow-up in-person visit can be scheduled if needed. They would also ask how the office includes the older adult in the conversation and confirms understanding.

**Example 4 — The accessibility question.**
*Hypothetical:* An older adult uses a mobility aid and is concerned about office access. The family might ask about entrance accessibility, seating, restrooms, and parking before committing to an in-person visit. If access is difficult, they might ask whether a remote meeting or an alternative location is possible.

In each example, the family's next step is the same: ask the office directly, compare answers across providers, and consult a qualified Elder Law Attorney for legal guidance.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Assuming remote is always easier.** Technology barriers can make remote meetings harder for some older adults.
- **Assuming in-person is always better.** Travel and scheduling burdens can outweigh the benefits for some families.
- **Skipping the test call.** A short test call can prevent a wasted appointment.
- **Forgetting to ask about documents.** Format choices often depend on what needs to be reviewed or signed.
- **Leaving the older adult out of the decision.** Their comfort and participation matter.
